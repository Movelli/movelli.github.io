    Global Connectivity for Digital Nomads

    Updated 11 October 2026|eSIM · VPN · Digital nomads · Travel hardware · Travel Wi-Fi

    Reliable internet is the one tool a remote worker cannot do without. This guide covers the layers of a connection that does not fail, the eSIM and VPN choices that go with it, how to keep your bank and your verification codes working, and the travel hardware worth carrying.

    Key takeaways

    • Build three layers: mobile data, a second route and an emergency option.
    • Install a travel eSIM before you fly, and keep your home number as a second line.
    • Use a VPN for public Wi-Fi, blocked websites and banking.
    • Carry a charger, a travel plug adapter and an extension cord.
    • Keep your power bank in your hand luggage.

    Build three layers of connection

    Nobody can promise that the network never fails, but you can make sure that one failure never stops your work. Think in three layers.

    1. Mobile data is your main connection: a travel eSIM or a local SIM, shared with your laptop as a hotspot.
    2. Wi-Fi is your second route: your accommodation, a coworking space or a café.
    3. An emergency option is for the day both fail: a second SIM on another network, an old phone with a second SIM or eSIM that you keep charged and use as a hotspot, or a place you can walk to that has a reliable connection.

    Each layer should work without the others. Test the one you plan to use before an important call, and know which one you would switch to. That habit prevents most dropped calls and missed deadlines.

    eSIM or physical SIM

    An eSIM is a SIM built into the phone. You install it with a QR code or a link, so you can buy a plan before you fly and have data the moment you land. A physical SIM is the card in the tray.

    Most recent phones take an eSIM, but an iPhone has to be an XS or later1, and a phone locked to its carrier may refuse a plan from another provider. Check before you buy, not at the airport.

    For a trip of a few weeks or more, an eSIM is the better choice: you set it up before you fly and you keep your home SIM. A physical SIM is the option when your phone does not support an eSIM.

    A local SIM card can be worth it for a long stay in one country, because you get a local number, and in some countries you can get a local SIM as an eSIM as well. Know the price: almost everywhere you have to register your personal details and show your passport, so the phone company of every country where you buy one holds your details. That is a privacy concern.

    Your bank may sell one too

    Some fintech banks sell a travel eSIM in their app, which lets you pay from the account you already use.

    Revolut (opens in a new tab) sells prepaid eSIM data plans under Lifestyle in its app, for single countries, regions or the world. You need an eSIM phone that is not carrier-locked, and your home number stays on the physical SIM.

    Wise (opens in a new tab) sells eSIM plans from the Travel hub of its app, for about 150 countries. Its global plans are valid for 12 months from the first connection, so keep the eSIM off until you land.

    The advantage of a bank is paying from the account you already use, not a lower price. Compare its plan with the providers above, and with a travel eSIM bought directly.

    Why unlimited slows down

    “Unlimited” on a travel eSIM rarely means the same speed all month. The network operator in the country applies a fair-usage policy, and the provider has to follow it. Holafly’s Thailand page, for example, says speed can be reduced in rare cases, for no more than a day, and does not say at what amount of data it starts.

    To avoid surprises:

    • Read the plan description for “fair use”, “daily”, “throttled” or “reduced speed” before you pay.
    • Look for a daily limit, not only the headline.
    • Check that sharing data with your laptop (hotspot or tethering) is allowed. Some plans limit it.

    If you rely on video calls, a fixed amount of data is the safer choice: the speed you pay for is the speed you get until the data is gone.

    Keep your bank and payment accounts working

    Banks watch for unusual logins. A new country, a public network and a VPN address that changes every day all look unusual, and an account can be locked at the worst moment.

    • Tell your bank or card issuer that you are travelling, if its app has a way to do that.
    • Log in once from a stable connection soon after you arrive, not for the first time from an airport network.
    • Keep your exit address the same. If you use a VPN, pick one server in the same country each time, or use a dedicated IP address that only you use.
    • Prefer an authenticator app or a security key over text-message codes.

    A dedicated IP address reduces the repeated checks that a changing address causes, but it does not guarantee that a bank will accept you.

    Keep receiving verification codes

    Many banks and apps still send login codes by text message to the number on file. If you swap your home SIM for a travel SIM, those messages go nowhere.

    The fix is a dual-SIM phone. Many recent phones, iPhone and Android, can keep two numbers active at once, a physical SIM plus an eSIM. On an iPhone, for example, only one number at a time uses cellular data1. Keep your home SIM in the tray, set the travel eSIM as your data line, and turn off data roaming on the home line so it does not use data abroad.

    Texts to your home number can still arrive, if your home carrier allows roaming for messages. Ask your carrier what that costs before you leave. If you have trouble receiving text messages, it can help to turn off automatic network selection on the home SIM and choose a carrier manually.

    Move your important accounts to an authenticator app as well, so a login does not depend on a phone number.

    Do you need a VPN, and which kind?

    A VPN encrypts the traffic between your device and the VPN server, so whoever runs a public network cannot read it. It also hides your real address from the sites you visit. It does not make you anonymous, and it does not make your connection faster.

    Nomads use one for four reasons:

    • safer public Wi-Fi;
    • reaching websites that the government of the country you stay in blocks;
    • using content from your home country that is geo-blocked abroad;
    • a stable address for banking.

    NordVPN (opens in a new tab) sells a dedicated IP as an add-on: an address reserved for you, usable on up to 10 devices at once with OpenVPN and on one with NordLynx2. It costs the same on every plan and, on a one-year plan, $5.89 a month.

    ExpressVPN (opens in a new tab) lets you connect up to 14 devices at once on its highest tier. A dedicated IP comes with that tier and is a separate purchase on the others. If a dedicated IP will not connect, ExpressVPN advises switching the protocol to Lightway.

    Surfshark (opens in a new tab) allows unlimited simultaneous connections. Its dedicated IP is a separate add-on from $3.75 a month, in 20 locations, and you cannot change the location after you get it.

    Proton VPN (opens in a new tab) has a free plan with no data limit, but it allows one connection at a time. Mullvad (opens in a new tab) costs 5 euros a month at any length of subscription (its page shows $5.60), works on up to five devices and takes anonymous payment, including cash by post.

    If you prefer to run your own, WireGuard is a modern VPN protocol designed to be easy to configure. Tailscale (opens in a new tab) builds on it to connect your own devices, and its personal plan is free for up to six users with unlimited devices.

    For public Wi-Fi, blocked websites and geo-blocked content, any of the commercial VPNs does the job. For banking, a dedicated IP is the extra to look at, and you pay for it. Run your own server only if you like to tinker.

    Wi-Fi in the places you stay

    Most nomads stay in homestays, rentals and coworking spaces, where the Wi-Fi is the host’s: you cannot change its speed or its router. On the first day, run a speed test and a video call before you rely on it for work, and keep mobile data as your second layer.

    Login pages in hotels, airports and trains

    Many public networks show a login page where you accept terms or enter a room number, and many places allow only a few devices per room. At airports, and maybe on trains, you cannot always log in on your laptop either. A phone connected to such a network may or may not be able to share it with your laptop as a hotspot (Wi-Fi sharing or internet sharing).

    What hardware to carry

    A few small things keep your devices charged and your connection running wherever you stay. This is the kit to pack, with a closer look at one charger and one travel plug adapter.

    The kit

    • A multi-port GaN charger
    • A travel plug adapter
    • An extension cord
    • A power bank, in your hand luggage
    • A travel router
    • A backup SIM or an old phone

    We recommend looking into the Anker Charger 100W with 3 Ports and Smart Display (opens in a new tab). It has two USB-C ports and one USB-A port, 100 W in total, and Anker lists the MacBook Air and Pro as compatible. It costs $69.99.

    Plug types differ per country, so a travel plug adapter is what makes your charger fit the wall socket. Anker makes a very small and light one, which weighs next to nothing in a bag.

    Bring a plain extension cord of about 10 feet (3 meters), not a power strip, which takes space and is heavy. A wall socket is often not where you want it, and a travel adapter with a heavy MacBook charger makes the plug fall out of the socket. On an extension cord, the charger can lie on a table or the floor.

    Keep your power bank in your hand luggage: spare lithium batteries and power banks may not travel in checked bags, and batteries of 101 to 160 Wh need approval from the airline3. Read the watt-hours on its label, not the milliamp-hours.

    When the connection fails

    Work through these steps in order. Most problems are solved in the first three.

    1. Check the basics: airplane mode is off, mobile data is on, and the travel eSIM is the line selected for data.
    2. Check data roaming. Most travel eSIMs need it switched on for the travel line.
    3. Restart the phone, then choose the network manually and try another one from the list.
    4. Open your provider’s app or help page and check whether the plan is active and has data left.
    5. Switch to your second layer: Wi-Fi, a hotspot from another phone, or your backup SIM.
    6. If nothing works, move to a place with a reliable connection, such as a coworking space, and tell your client that you will be back online shortly.

    Pre-trip checklist

    • Check that your phone supports eSIM and is not locked to a carrier.
    • Buy and install your travel eSIM while you still have a connection, and switch it on when you land.
    • Keep your home SIM active as a second line, and turn off data roaming on it.
    • Move important accounts from text-message codes to an authenticator app.
    • Tell your bank you are travelling, if its app lets you.
    • Install your VPN app and sign in before you leave.
    • Pack your charger, travel plug adapter and extension cord.
    • Charge your power bank and pack it in your hand luggage.
    • Download offline maps and your booking details.
    • Save your provider’s support contact where you can read it offline.

    Still have questions?

    Quick answers to common questions about staying connected while you travel and work.
    Can I use my home carrier abroad instead?

    You can, but roaming charges and limits vary a lot between carriers and plans. Check your plan’s roaming terms before you leave, and compare them with a travel eSIM for the countries you will visit.

    Is hotel Wi-Fi safe for banking?

    Use your mobile data or a VPN for banking when you can. If you have to use the hotel network, make sure the app or site uses HTTPS and keep your devices updated.

    Can I share my phone’s data with my laptop?

    Yes, on most phones this is the hotspot or tethering setting. Check that your plan allows it, because some plans limit or block it.

    How much data do I need?

    It depends on how you work. Email and chat use little, and video calls use much more. Count your call hours, then pick a plan with room to spare or one that you can top up.

    Does a VPN slow my connection down?

    Usually a little, because your traffic takes a detour through the VPN server. A server close to you keeps the difference small.
