    Most people only think about mobile data when it has already stopped working: the plane lands, the phone finds a network it does not recognise, and a message appears about roaming charges. A little planning before the trip turns that moment into a non-event. This guide walks through the choices in the order you will actually meet them, from the phone in your pocket to the first morning in a new country.

    Text

    The first thing to find out is whether your phone can use an eSIM at all. Most phones sold in the last four years can, but carrier-locked models often cannot take a second plan until the lock is removed. Open the settings, look for a line called the EID or a mention of a digital SIM, and write down what you find while you still have a connection.

    The second thing is the lock itself. A phone bought on a monthly plan is frequently tied to the carrier that sold it, and unlocking can take a few days. Request it early, because a request made at the airport will not be answered before you board.

    Unlocked, locked and in between

    A fully unlocked phone accepts any SIM or eSIM from any provider, which is what you want. A locked phone only accepts the carrier that sold it, and some carriers unlock automatically once the device is paid off. If you are unsure, the quickest test is to put in a SIM from another network and see whether it is recognised.

    Phones bought abroad add a further wrinkle, because the radio bands can differ between regions. A model sold in North America may miss a band that is common in Europe or Asia, so coverage can be thinner than the map suggests. The manufacturer's specification page lists the bands, and a provider's coverage page lists the ones it uses.

    What the settings screen tells you

    On most phones the relevant information sits under a menu named for the network, the SIM or the general device information. Look for a line saying the phone is unlocked or that there are no restrictions on the SIM. If the wording is vague, a screenshot of the screen is enough for a provider's support team to answer.

    It also helps to note the model number and the software version while you are there. Providers occasionally ask for both, and the answer decides whether a plan can be installed at all. Keep the screenshot in a place you can open without a connection.

    Small details that save a support call

    Write the order number and the email address you used on a card or a note that is not stored on the phone. If the phone is the thing that fails, you will want that information somewhere else. A paper copy in the passport sleeve costs nothing and has rescued many a first evening.

    Do the same for the plan's activation details, such as the code or the address you were sent. Some providers send them once and never again, so a lost message can mean a new purchase. Take a photo of the screen as soon as it appears.

    One last thing before you leave the house

    Turn off automatic updates for apps while you are on a metered plan, and download the maps and the tickets you will need for the first day. The phone will then behave itself on its first hour abroad, and you can spend that hour looking around instead of watching a progress bar.

    FAQ

    Does this page appear in search results?

    Not once the site is live. This is a demo page for testing only, and it is removed before launch.

    Why is there text between the components?

    Because a component is almost never seen alone. Judging the spacing and the type needs the paragraph above and the heading below, not a component on a blank page.

    Is an FAQ still worth having?

    As a block on a page that earns it, yes. It still feeds AI answer engines and our own structured data, even though Google withdrew FAQ rich results in May 2026.

    Banners

    A banner is a band with a picture behind it: a real <img> from the media library, with alt text and its renditions. Its title, text and any button or form are components in its slot, set to the inverse look for the dark overlay. Its shape is a variant, named for the width it is made for. In the full-width layout, with the variant full, the photo runs edge to edge with square corners: 4/3 on a phone, 16/7, then 21/9 once the banner is 60rem wide. Its components sit in a box of its contentWidth (the reading width, or here the wide one), centred at the foot of the band.

    Layouts

    A page is a stack of layouts. Each layout is a web component with its own markup and its own CSS (its slots, its widths, its gaps and where it changes), and the components in each of its slots stack. Everything below is a layout from the layout catalog, and the space above each one is the layout's own.

    Two columns

    Two equal halves at the wide width, side by side from a 62rem window, where each half is just wider than a paragraph's minimum width, with a spacious gap between them; stacked below that, the first first.

    What works

    • Installed before you fly, working the moment you land, with no queue and no kiosk.
    • No passport photocopy at a kiosk.
    • Keeps your home number live for two-factor codes.

    What doesn't

    • Usually dearer per gigabyte than a local SIM.
    • Data only, with no local number for taxis or deliveries.
    • Older phones cannot use it at all.
